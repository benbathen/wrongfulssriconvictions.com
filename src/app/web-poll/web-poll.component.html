47 prosecutors at SDCDA, the office that prosecuted him vote "No" in response to the question, "Was Ben Bathen guilty of three counts of PC 422 criminal threats?"

Although 47 prosectors in the San Diego District Attorney's office voted not guilty in an online web poll after viewing the FDA Hearing videos, to date none of the prosecutors have adhered to the special duties of a prosecutor which requires them to perform a re-investigation and assist in remedying the conviction.

Ethical duties of San Diego County Deputy District Attorneys James Joel Madero and Cherie Brianne Somerville, and other SDCDA prosecutors, after admitting Mr. Bathen is not guilty

Legal Memorandum

I. Question Presented

Whether it is ethically permissible for San Diego County prosecutors who have now affirmatively indicated that Benjamin Lee Bathen is not guilty of three counts of Penal Code § 422 to take no steps to correct or remedy his conviction, where

  1. The conviction rests on the perjured and financially interested testimony of Dr. Carolyn F. Jacobs
  2. The prosecutors previously suppressed impeachment and scientific evidence<>
  3. Forty-seven SDCDA lawyers – including trial prosecutors James Joel Madero and Cherie Brianne Somerville and the Conviction & Sentence Review Unit (CASRU) – answered “No” to the question, “Was Benjamin Bathen guilty of three counts of PC 422 criminal threats?” in an August 4, 2023 web poll.

II. Brief Answer

No. Under California’s Rules of Professional Conduct, the Business and Professions Code, and nationally recognized standards such as ABA Model Rule 3.8(g)–(h), prosecutors are “ministers of justice” whose duty is not merely to win convictions but to ensure that innocent persons are not wrongfully punished. Once a prosecutor knows of new, credible and material evidence creating at least a reasonable likelihood that a convicted defendant did not commit the offense – and especially once the prosecutor personally believes the defendant is not guilty – the prosecutor has an affirmative duty to disclose that information, to facilitate further investigation, and, when appropriate, to seek relief from the conviction rather than oppose it.

In People v. Bathen, the conviction was obtained by (a) presenting and exploiting Dr. Jacobs’s materially false testimony that she had “no contact” with Mr. Bathen for more than eight years and that therapy lawfully ended when he left California, and (b) concealing her $25,100 restitution claim that effectively paid her for investigating and testifying as a witness. These actions violated the duties of candor, disclosure, and fairness at trial. The later web-poll admissions by forty-seven SDCDA prosecutors – after reviewing the SSRI evidence, impeachment materials, and Mr. Bathen’s post-conviction filings – are “new, credible, and material evidence” that the office itself now recognizes his innocence.

In that posture, for the same prosecutors to remain silent, to continue opposing relief, or to allow a plainly wrongful felony conviction to stand is inconsistent with California Rules of Professional Conduct 3.3, 3.4, 3.8 and 8.4, Business & Professions Code §§ 6068(a) & (d) and 6106, and the principles of Berger v. United States, Napue v. Illinois, and Brady v. Maryland. Their inaction transforms past misconduct into an ongoing ethical violation.

III. Factual Background

A. The 2018 Trial and Dr. Jacobs’s Central Role

The prosecution of People v. Benjamin Lee Bathen, San Diego County Superior Court Case No. SCS294342, was based almost entirely on three voicemails left for Dr. Carolyn F. Jacobs in 2017. There was no physical evidence and no third-party eyewitness to the calls; the case turned on whether the jury believed Dr. Jacobs’s fear was genuine and whether Mr. Bathen possessed the specific intent required by Penal Code § 422.

At trial, Dr. Jacobs – both the complaining witness and a licensed psychologist – was presented as a neutral, unbiased victim with no ulterior motive. The prosecutors elicited testimony that after 2008 she had “no contact with [Mr. Bathen] for over eight years” and that she “could not continue treating him after he left California,” implying therapy ended lawfully and cleanly in 2008. She further told the jury, “I had not seen or spoken with him in over eight years,” portraying the 2017 calls as coming “out of nowhere.”

These statements were materially false. Contemporaneous emails show that Dr. Jacobs continued communicating with Mr. Bathen in 2008–2009, offering new appointment dates and arranging interstate telephonic sessions while he was living in Massachusetts, despite not being licensed there. Police reports and Jacobs’s own notes reflect her concern that this conduct might be reported as illegal out-of-state practice – a fact that gave her a personal motive to depict Mr. Bathen as dangerous rather than as a former patient raising legitimate complaints.

B. Secret Financial Inducements and Restitution

Before sentencing, Dr. Jacobs submitted a sworn “Summary of Economic Losses” requesting $25,100 in restitution under Penal Code § 1202.4. Her claim included payment for “pre-trial meetings with District Attorneys,” “on call to testify in the trial,” hours spent “testifying as [a] witness,” attending closing arguments, and attending the verdict – in total, more than $11,000 for her own time plus $12,400 for her husband’s time.

At trial, however, Dr. Jacobs did not tell the jury she expected to be compensated for these activities. Instead, she portrayed her involvement as purely motivated by safety and justice, never hinting at lost income or financial stake. The prosecution did not disclose the restitution request or its preparation, thereby concealing a powerful source of impeachment: Dr. Jacobs’s financial incentive to secure a conviction.

C. Improper Closing Argument Built on False Testimony

In closing argument, the prosecutor capitalized on this misleading picture. She described the case to the jury by stating, “Dr. Jacobs was terrorized by her former patient… I ask you to hold him accountable… find him guilty.” She asserted that “he doesn’t get to hide behind a phone and leave sadistic, horrifying messages that terrorized Dr. Jacobs,” using inflammatory language untethered from any expert evaluation of Mr. Bathen’s medication-induced mental state.

To rebut the defense suggestion that Dr. Jacobs had acted unethically in continuing to treat Mr. Bathen across state lines, the prosecutor told the jury that “[defense says] she’s unethical… [but] she felt an ethical obligation to continue care… She didn’t just drop him,” reinforcing the false narrative that treatment ended properly in 2008 and that Jacobs had no motive to lie.

D. Scientific and Medical Evidence of SSRI-Induced Aggression and Akathisia

Post-conviction filings and exhibits document that at the time of the voicemails Mr. Bathen was taking the SSRI escitalopram (Lexapro) and experiencing classic akathisia and emerging tardive dyskinesia – movement disorders and internal agitation strongly associated with antidepressant-induced suicidality and aggression. A major systematic review of clinical study reports found that in children and adolescents selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors and SNRIs produced “a doubling of both suicidality and aggression” compared with placebo.1 Other peer-reviewed literature and expert monographs describe how SSRIs can create “medication spellbinding,” in which patients are unable to perceive the drug-induced changes in their own thinking and behavior, including violence and self-harm.2 Additional work by Gøtzsche and colleagues critiques the under-reporting of severe psychiatric adverse events and concludes that antidepressants can cause serious violence in a subset of patients.3

FAERS and FOIA data for escitalopram, compiled in the Lexapro Detailed Report and related homicide-case spreadsheets, show multiple reports of completed homicides, suicide–homicides, and severe aggression temporally associated with Lexapro treatment. A survey of criminal cases worldwide confirms that courts have repeatedly credited antidepressant-induced violence as a mitigating or exculpatory factor, including cases where defendants were acquitted or received substantially reduced sentences after proof that SSRIs contributed to otherwise inexplicable violent acts.

E. Post-Trial Discovery of Prosecutorial Misconduct

Subsequent investigation for post-conviction relief uncovered Dr. Jacobs’s restitution claim, police reports referencing her concern about being reported for practicing across state lines, and email correspondence contradicting her “no contact” testimony. These materials formed the basis of a Penal Code § 1473.7 motion to vacate the conviction on grounds of actual innocence and ineffective assistance of counsel, which also included scientific documentation of SSRI-induced aggression and Mr. Bathen’s vulnerable CYP450 genotype.

F. August 4, 2023 Web Poll – Prosecutorial Admissions of Actual Innocence

On August 4, 2023, after links were circulated to the FDA hearing transcripts, FAERS analyses, and Mr. Bathen’s case materials, forty-seven prosecutors at the San Diego County District Attorney’s Office responded to an internal Constant Contact poll asking: “Was Benjamin Bathen guilty of three counts of PC 422 criminal threats?” The overwhelming majority – forty-seven respondents – answered “No.” The list of “No” responses includes Deputy District Attorney James Joel Madero (joel.madero@sdccda.org) and Deputy District Attorney Cherie Brianne Somerville (cherie.somerville@sdccda.org), the very prosecutors who tried and convicted Mr. Bathen, as well as casru@sdccda.org, the Conviction and Sentence Review Unit.

These poll responses are post-trial admissions by officers of the court that Mr. Bathen was wrongfully convicted. Combined with the underlying scientific and impeachment evidence, they easily meet – and likely exceed – the “reasonable likelihood” and “clear and convincing evidence” thresholds used in modern ethical rules governing prosecutors’ post-conviction duties.

IV. Governing Ethical and Legal Standards

A. Prosecutor as “Minister of Justice”

The United States Supreme Court has long emphasized that a prosecutor “is the representative not of an ordinary party to a controversy, but of a sovereignty… whose interest… in a criminal prosecution is not that it shall win a case, but that justice shall be done.” Berger v. United States, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935). California law echoes this principle: prosecutors must “seek justice within the bounds of the law, not merely to convict.” See Cal. R. Prof. Conduct 3.8 cmt. [1]; Bus. & Prof. Code § 6068(a), (d).

B. Duties of Candor, Disclosure, and Fairness at Trial

  • Rule 3.3 (Candor Toward the Tribunal). A lawyer who comes to know that a witness called by the lawyer has offered material false evidence must take “reasonable remedial measures,” including disclosure to the court if necessary. Cal. R. Prof. Conduct 3.3(a)(3).
  • Rule 3.4(d) (Witness Compensation). A lawyer shall not “directly or indirectly, pay or acquiesce in the payment of compensation to a witness contingent upon the content of the witness’s testimony or the outcome of the case.” Cal. R. Prof. Conduct 3.4(d).
  • Rule 3.8 (Special Responsibilities of a Prosecutor). California’s version requires prosecutors to make timely disclosure of all evidence or information known to them that tends to negate guilt or mitigate punishment and to exercise reasonable care to prevent law enforcement agents from making improper extrajudicial statements. Cal. R. Prof. Conduct 3.8(d), (f).
  • Business & Professions Code § 6068(d). Every attorney must “employ, for the purpose of maintaining the causes confided to him or her, those means only as are consistent with truth, and never seek to mislead the judge or any judicial officer by an artifice or false statement of fact or law.”
  • Business & Professions Code § 6106. Acts of dishonesty or moral turpitude – including knowingly presenting false testimony or suppressing material facts – warrant discipline up to disbarment.

Constitutional case law reinforces these obligations. Under Napue v. Illinois, 360 U.S. 264 (1959), “a conviction obtained through use of false evidence, known to be such by representatives of the State, must fall under the Fourteenth Amendment,” and the rule applies even when the falsehood relates only to a witness’s credibility. Under Brady v. Maryland, 373 U.S. 83 (1963), prosecutors must disclose evidence favorable to the accused that is material to guilt or punishment. California decisions such as People v. Phillips, 41 Cal. 3d 29, 46 (1985), and In re Sakarias, 35 Cal. 4th 140, 159 (2005), hold that intentionally suppressing material impeachment evidence or knowingly permitting false testimony constitutes a denial of due process and professional misconduct.

C. Post-Conviction Duties Under Modern Prosecutorial Ethics

ABA Model Rule 3.8(g)–(h) codifies the now widely recognized duty of prosecutors to act when they learn of possible wrongful convictions. It provides that when a prosecutor knows of new, credible, and material evidence creating a reasonable likelihood that a convicted defendant did not commit the offense, the prosecutor must promptly disclose that evidence to an appropriate court or authority and undertake, or make reasonable efforts to cause, further investigation. When a prosecutor knows of clear and convincing evidence establishing that a defendant was convicted of an offense the defendant did not commit, the prosecutor must seek to remedy the conviction. Model Rules of Prof’l Conduct r. 3.8(g)–(h) (Am. Bar Ass’n 2020).

Although California has not yet formally added subsections (g)–(h) to its own Rule 3.8, California ethics commentary and national case law increasingly look to these provisions as persuasive authority on a prosecutor’s continuing duty to do justice. For example, in In re Kline, 113 A.3d 202 (D.C. 2015), a prosecutor was disciplined for failing to disclose exculpatory evidence post-trial, with the court emphasizing that intentional or reckless non-disclosure violates both Rule 3.8 and the general prohibition against conduct prejudicial to the administration of justice.

San Diego’s own Conviction and Sentence Review Unit (CASRU) is premised on these national standards. When CASRU and the line prosecutors themselves have reviewed the evidence and concluded that a prior conviction is erroneous, their ethical obligations are at their apex, not their minimum.

D. California’s Actual-Innocence Remedy – Penal Code § 1473.7

Penal Code § 1473.7 authorizes a motion to vacate a conviction where new evidence establishes actual innocence or where prejudicial error damaged the defendant’s ability to meaningfully understand, defend against, or knowingly accept the adverse immigration consequences of a plea. In Mr. Bathen’s case, a detailed § 1473.7 motion has already been filed, marshalling the impeachment evidence against Dr. Jacobs, the scientific evidence regarding SSRI-induced akathisia and aggression, and expert DNA analysis of his CYP450 metabolism. Prosecutors faced with such a motion are not ordinary adversaries; they are ethically bound to reevaluate the conviction in good faith and, where persuaded of innocence, to consent to or affirmatively support vacatur rather than reflexively oppose it.

V. Analysis: Why Continued Inaction Is Unethical and warrants suspension or dsbarment from the legal profession

A. The Poll Responses Are “New, Credible, and Material Evidence” of Wrongful Conviction

The August 4, 2023 Constant Contact poll asked a clear, binary question: “Was Benjamin Bathen guilty of three counts of PC 422 criminal threats?” Forty-seven prosecutors – including the trial prosecutors and CASRU – answered “No.” These responses were made after the respondents had access to the trial transcript, FDA hearing materials, FAERS data on Lexapro, Dr. Jacobs’s restitution request, and detailed post-conviction analyses of medication-induced violence and akathisia.

Under ABA Model Rule 3.8(g), the combination of (1) serious scientific evidence that Lexapro can precipitate uncharacteristic violent ideation, (2) proof that Dr. Jacobs lied about her continuing treatment and her financial inducement, and (3) the prosecutors’ own considered judgment that Mr. Bathen was not guilty, unquestionably qualifies as “new, credible, and material evidence” creating at least a reasonable likelihood he did not commit the offense as charged. Indeed, the evidence rises to “clear and convincing” under Rule 3.8(h): the primary witness’s testimony has been impeached by documentary evidence, her financial stake has been exposed, the psychiatric science points away from mens rea, and the prosecuting attorneys themselves no longer believe in the conviction.

In this posture, prosecutors cannot ethically remain passive. To do nothing is to knowingly permit an innocent person to bear the consequences of a wrongful felony conviction – a result the Model Rules expressly forbid.

B. Continuing to Stand on a Conviction They Know Is False Violates Rules 3.3, 3.8, and § 6068(d)

At trial, the prosecutors allowed Dr. Jacobs to testify falsely that she had “no contact” with Mr. Bathen after 2008 and could not lawfully treat him once he left California, despite documentary evidence in the case file showing continued contact and interstate teletherapy. Rule 3.3(a)(3) required them to correct that false testimony once it emerged; they did not. Under Napue, this alone rendered the conviction constitutionally unreliable.

They also concealed Dr. Jacobs’s restitution claim and financial interest, a classic Brady violation and breach of Rule 3.8(d) and § 6068(d), which forbid suppression of material facts that could impeach a key witness. The Daily Journal ethics analysis – cited in the existing State Bar complaint – specifically warns that prosecutors must disclose victim compensation and restitution expectations when those witnesses testify.

Those violations did not end with the verdict. By opposing or failing to support Mr. Bathen’s § 1473.7 motion even after the restitution documents and emails came to light, and by staying silent after they personally concluded he is not guilty, the prosecutors continue to mislead courts and parole authorities about the integrity of the conviction. Each filing that defends the verdict without disclosing their current belief in his innocence is an “artifice or false statement of fact or law” within the meaning of § 6068(d).

C. The Duty to Seek Justice Requires Supporting, Not Opposing, Relief

Under the prosecutor-as-minister-of-justice standard, once Madero, Somerville, and CASRU concluded that the evidence, science, and post-trial record demonstrate Mr. Bathen’s actual innocence, their ethical duty shifted from defending the verdict to correcting it. At a minimum, they must:

  • Formally disclose their changed assessment of guilt, and the basis for it, to Mr. Bathen and to the courts currently reviewing his § 1473.7 motion;
  • Provide the poll results and all impeachment materials to any reviewing court or agency considering his conviction, parole, or employment background;
  • Cease filing oppositions that assert or imply they still believe he is guilty or that the trial was fair; and
  • Affirmatively support vacatur of the conviction, whether by confessing error, stipulating to relief, or joining a motion to vacate and recall sentence.

Refusing to take these steps would amount to the very misconduct condemned in Sakarias – “knowingly permitting or arguing false testimony” – now in a post-conviction context. It would also be prejudicial to the administration of justice under Rule 8.4(d), because it keeps an innocent person branded as a violent felon while the prosecuting authority quietly acknowledges his innocence in private.

D. Public Policy and the Integrity of the SDCDA

Beyond rule-book formalities, the legitimacy of the San Diego County District Attorney’s Office depends on the public’s belief that prosecutors will act when they discover a wrongful conviction – not merely when litigation pressure forces them to. Wrongful conviction cases across the country commonly involve suppressed impeachment evidence, medication-induced mental states, and reliance on a single, compromised witness. The Bathen case has all of these features, and the office’s own prosecutors have already, in effect, voted that it is a miscarriage of justice.

If, in spite of that, SDCDA leaves Mr. Bathen’s conviction untouched, the message to the public and to future defendants is that truth and innocence are subordinate to institutional pride. That outcome is flatly at odds with Berger’s command that the sovereign’s interest is that “justice shall be done,” and it is inconsistent with the national trend toward conviction review units that are empowered – and ethically obligated – to rectify wrongs rather than entrench them.

VI. Conclusion

The combination of

  1. Dr. Jacobs’s proven perjury and undisclosed financial inducements
  2. powerful scientific evidence that escitalopram can precipitate violent ideation in vulnerable patients
  3. the documented pattern of Brady and Napue violations at trial
  4. the August 4, 2023 web-poll admissions by forty-seven SDCDA prosecutors – including the trial prosecutors and CASRU – establishes at least clear and convincing evidence that Benjamin Lee Bathen did not commit the crime for which he was convicted. Under California’s Rules of Professional Conduct, the Business and Professions Code, and persuasive national standards embodied in ABA Model Rule 3.8(g)–(h), it is unethical for those prosecutors to do nothing.

Their duty now is to disclose, investigate, and remedy. That means supporting Mr. Bathen’s § 1473.7 motion to vacate, informing the courts of their assessment of his innocence, and taking whatever steps are necessary to remove a conviction they know is false. Anything less is inconsistent with their oath, their role as ministers of justice, and the fundamental promise of our criminal justice system that the innocent will not be punished.
